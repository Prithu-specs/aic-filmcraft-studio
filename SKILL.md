# AIC Filmcraft Studio

Use this skill when the request involves planning, generating, reviewing, editing, or delivering an AI film, short film, trailer, proof reel, music video, narrative video, or video sequence. It is the default studio workflow for Filmcraft productions.

## Mission

Make every production emotionally clear, visually consistent, financially deliberate, and technically deliverable. The story comes before the tool. A generated clip is only a candidate until it passes its intended review.

Work in this order:

1. Select the production mode.
2. Establish the creative contract and authority boundaries.
3. Build the story and character engine.
4. Lock the canon and register assets.
5. Turn scenes into controlled packets and generate only after preflight.
6. Review clips, joins, and sound before assembling the next dependency.
7. Finish, verify, deliver, archive, and capture the lesson.

Use [production modes](references/production-modes.md) to choose the right depth. Use [working templates](references/templates.md) to create the production records. Use [roles and quality controls](references/roles-and-quality.md) when the project has more than one scene, a team, significant cost, a competition, or a public release. See the [studio coverage map](references/studio-map.md) for the workflow integration.

## 1. Select a production mode

| Mode | Runtime | Apply |
|---|---:|---|
| Mini | 30 seconds–3 minutes | concise brief, story turn, compact canon, shot cards, mandatory preflight/review/delivery checks |
| Short | 3–15 minutes | full scene packets, asset and clip ledgers, continuity and seam review, picture lock, sound pass |
| Long | More than 15 minutes | sequence-level planning, selected specialist coverage, change control, weekly recovery and archive checks |

Scale documentation to risk. Do not apply all specialist departments to a simple video. Do not skip the creative brief, canon, preflight, review, rights, or master checks because a project is small.

## 2. Set the creative contract

Create a brief before creating prompts or buying credits. Record:

- audience, platform, desired reaction, runtime, format, deadline, spending ceiling, and delivery target;
- logline; beginning, change, and consequence; emotional promise; turn/payoff; and one key visual;
- available tools and their verified current capability, cost, duration, reference, export, and commercial-use limits;
- source, likeness, music, voice, and asset permissions; and
- what may be decided autonomously, what requires producer approval, and who has the final release decision.

Treat a tool route as a current hypothesis, not a permanent fact. Check the actual tool interface and plan limits before every paid run. Never represent a capability, price, rights status, or generated output as verified without evidence.

## 3. Build the character-led story engine

For narrative work, every principal character needs an active dramatic function. Establish:

- **want:** what they pursue now;
- **pressure:** what makes the pursuit costly or difficult;
- **private contradiction:** belief, fear, habit, or blind spot that complicates it;
- **visible behavior:** a choice, action, or silence that lets viewers infer character;
- **change:** what is different by the end; and
- **relationship vector:** how each key relationship changes scene by scene.

Write from behavior and consequence. Let dialogue, decisions, objects, blocking, and reactions reveal character. Avoid narrator exposition that repeats what the scene already shows.

For non-narrative work, replace the character engine with an audience progression: immediate question, useful tension, evidence or demonstration, change in understanding, and clear next action.

Before visual planning, deliver a beat map whose scenes each have an incoming state, one dominant change, and an outgoing state. Time dialogue, silence, reactions, titles, and transitions. Each scene must earn its place by changing information, emotion, or action.

## 4. Build and lock the visual canon

Create a versioned canon before production. It is the single source of truth for characters, voices, costume, props, locations, time, camera language, lighting, color, sound, typography, aspect ratio, and prohibited drift.

Register every reference asset with its file path or URL, version, source/rights status, scope, and approval state. Separate fixed details from scene-specific variables:

- **Fixed block:** identity, wardrobe, prop design, location rules, light and color rules, camera grammar, sound rules, aspect ratio, and exclusion list.
- **Variable block:** the current action, emotion, framing, movement, time, and scene-specific sound event.

A material change to a locked item creates a new canon version and an impact list of affected scenes, assets, prompts, audio, edit, and approvals. Do not silently overwrite the active canon.

## 5. Turn a scene into a controlled packet

Create one scene packet for each scene; create a shot card for each generated unit. Include only the department decisions the scene needs. At minimum capture:

- scene intent, viewer understanding, emotional direction, and one primary action;
- incoming/outgoing character, prop, costume, geography, light, and sound state;
- timed beat plan, performance and blocking, framing, lens/perspective intent, camera movement, lighting, and sound role;
- exact approved references and canon version;
- shot acceptance criteria, continuity risk, fallback method, estimated cost, and owner; and
- handoff state: planned, ready, generating, review, approved, repair, retired, or stale.

Build coverage for the edit, not merely attractive images: establish geography; show the causal action; preserve a reaction or hold where the cut needs one; and plan each entrance, exit, prop handoff, and sound bridge.

## 6. Preflight and generate with control

Before each attempt, verify the current tool, model, duration, aspect ratio, reference method, seed/settings where available, safety/rights fit, remaining budget, input assets, output destination, and the specific acceptance test. Spend first on the defining or riskiest shot at a reduced test scale when possible.

Record attempt ID, source prompt/settings, canon and asset versions, tool/model, inputs, cost or credit estimate, output location, result, decision, defect, and next action. Preserve rejected attempts as evidence rather than losing the reason a method failed.

After two similar failures, stop repeating the same variation. Diagnose the failure, then change one meaningful variable: reference method, staging, shot design, tool route, or simplify the action while preserving the story meaning. Do not disguise an exhausted retry budget under a new task name.

## 7. Review before you continue

Run review in separate lanes:

1. **Creative:** story clarity, performance, emotional direction, composition, rhythm.
2. **Continuity:** character identity, wardrobe, props, geography, action direction, screen direction, lighting, sound state.
3. **Technical:** duration, frame integrity, artifacts, lip sync if relevant, export specs, readable titles.
4. **Rights and release:** asset provenance, consent, music/voice/license terms, platform requirements, captions/accessibility.

Rate the retained interval, not a flattering still. Review every join in motion with sound. A clip may be approved only if it has a named reviewer, stated evidence, and an intended use. Unverified is not approved.

For repair, issue a targeted request: preserve these locked details; correct this observable defect; make no unrelated changes; test against this acceptance criterion. For a material creative shift, open change control instead of treating it as a repair.

## 8. Edit, sound, finish, deliver, archive

Edit as a second writing pass. Build the strongest causal order; remove repetition; use reaction, silence, sound bridges, and shot duration to shape meaning. Lock picture before final mix and finishing unless a documented change reopens it.

Plan sound deliberately: dialogue/voice, ambience, Foley, designed effects, music, silence, transitions, and mix priorities. Music must support the scene turn rather than announce emotion too early. Confirm all voice and music rights before release.

Before delivery, verify the master by decoding and watching/listening through it. Check framing, captions, titles, sync, audio, color, credits, metadata, export format, rights, and destination-specific requirements. Save delivery proof or receipt.

Archive the active brief, canon, scene packets, source assets, approved clips, review records, edit project, audio stems, master, delivery record, and a short lesson: what worked, what failed, what to reuse, and what to avoid. Test that a teammate can recover the project from the archive.

## Production controls

Use these controls on every project:

- The project owner defines the approval envelope. No contributor or AI role self-approves release, spending beyond budget, rights, or a material creative change.
- Pin one active version for each brief, script, canon, packet, cut, and master. Candidates do not replace active records until an integrator accepts them.
- Keep a portable ledger with status, owner, evidence, decision, issue, and next action at project, scene, shot, attempt, clip, seam, cut, sound, master, and destination scope.
- Keep an edit, QA, upload, and contingency reserve rather than spending the entire budget on generation.
- Use the smallest useful tool stack. Verify current vendor claims directly; never hard-code stale provider prices or limits.

## Department and role routing

For a solo production, use separate passes rather than inventing a large team: writer, director, art/continuity, cinematography/lighting, sound, editor, producer/QA. For a team or long project, assign clear responsibility and a handoff artifact.

Select specialist depth only when it changes the result: story, production, performance, concept/art/props/appearance, camera/lighting, storyboard/continuity, model feasibility/generation/assets/VFX, editorial, dialogue/Foley/effects/ambience/music/mix, finish/QC/accessibility, rights/delivery/archive, and physical-action applicability.

Never parallelize conflicting edits to the same active artifact. Parallel research and candidate creation may proceed from a pinned brief/canon; an assigned integrator reconciles them against the current version.
